Part A · Methodological appendix

A1Overview

The index answers one question: what does the same Thanksgiving dinner for 10 cost each October, and how does that cost compare with prices, wages and household budgets? It holds the menu and quantities fixed and prices each item each year. October is used because it is the last BLS average-price month before the holiday and the month the American Farm Bureau Federation (AFBF) survey is closest to.

Step 1

Fix the menu

18 items in set quantities, built on the AFBF menu and expanded to a fuller meal.

Step 2

Price each item

BLS average prices where they exist; a 2026 anchor price moved back with the closest CPI component where they don't.

Step 3

Rebuild turkey

BLS stopped the turkey series after Feb 2020; a backtested ensemble estimates it from series still published.

Step 4

Localize and compare

NYC prices from a New York Post store survey, and comparisons with CPI, wages and Consumer Expenditure Survey budgets.

Headline results this appendix supports: the national dinner costs $87.39 in 2026 ($42.01 in 1995, $66.73 in 2019, $87.22 in 2025). The NYC version costs $167.77 ($141.28 in 2025, $109.16 in 2019).

A3Item prices

BLS average prices

Six items (and turkey through 2019) come straight from the BLS Average Price Data (CPI program), downloaded from FRED as monthly U.S. city averages. The October value is used.

Anchor-and-index backcasting

For items BLS does not price, a single observed 2026 price is carried back through time with the CPI component that best matches the item:

price(item, y) = anchor(item, 2026) × CPI_c(October y) / CPI_c(October 2026)

where c is the item's CPI component. This keeps each item's 2026 level fixed to a real price and lets its history follow the measured price trend of its group. It assumes the item's price moved in proportion to its group's index, which is the main source of imputation error (B2).

Missing months and series breaks

  • Shutdown months. BLS did not publish October 2013 (turkey) or October 2025 (all items) because of federal shutdowns. The October value is the average of September and November; where one is also missing (russet potatoes lack November 2025), the month that exists is used.
  • Butter 2012–18. The BLS butter series has no observations in these years. Values are bridged from the last observed price with the dairy CPI.

A4Turkey reconstruction

Turkey is about 39% of the dinner's cost, and BLS stopped publishing its retail price (APU0000706311, whole frozen, per lb) after February 2020. Any recent dinner total therefore depends on an estimate. turkey_price_ml_modeling.py estimates March 2020 to October 2026 from series that are still published, and chooses its method by how well each candidate predicts known prices under conditions that mimic the real gap.

Target and predictors

The model predicts the turkey price relative to grocery prices, then converts back to dollars:

target(t) = log( turkey(t) / CPI_food_at_home(t) ) turkey_hat(t) = exp( prediction(t) ) × CPI_food_at_home(t)

Modeling the ratio removes the general inflation trend so models learn turkey-specific swings. All predictors are monthly and divided by the food-at-home CPI:

Backtest design

Real-time conditions are recreated four times. Each backtest trains on data from January 1993 through February of 2004, 2007, 2010 or 2013, then predicts up to 80 months ahead (the length of the real gap, Mar 2020–Oct 2026) using only the predictors, never the turkey prices in the test window. Predictions are scored against actual BLS prices. This produces 2,219 forecast errors by model, origin and horizon.

Disclosure. The first selection rule picked the single best model by October error; the winner changed with small specification changes, which showed the ranking was noise. The equal-weight combination was added afterwards. It also ranks first under the original rule, but its choice was made after seeing backtest results.

Uncertainty band

The 80% band is empirical: for each horizon year, the 10th and 90th percentiles of the combined model's backtest errors are applied to the estimate. It assumes future errors look like past ones.

Items not in the Post survey

The seven remaining items (russet potatoes, peas, green beans, pie crusts, whipping cream, eggs, sugar) use the national price times an NYC price factor:

factor(y) = RPP_goods(NY metro, 2024)/100 × [ CPI_FAH,NY(y) / CPI_FAH,US(y) ] / [ CPI_FAH,NY(2024) / CPI_FAH,US(2024) ]

BEA's Regional Price Parity for goods in the New York–Newark–Jersey City metro (RPPGOOD35620) is 110.3 in 2024. It is moved to other years by the ratio of the New York and U.S. food-at-home CPIs (annual averages, CUURA101SAF11 vs CUUR0000SAF11). The factor is 1.121 in 1995, 1.136 in 2019, 1.108 in 2025 and 1.118 in 2026.

A6Index numbers and contributions

Two indexes compare the basket with general prices (chart 2): a fixed-basket Laspeyres index, which is simply total cost relative to a base year, and a geometric (Cobb-Douglas) index that weights items by expenditure share and allows for substitution:

Laspeyres(y) = Σ_i q_i p_i(y) / Σ_i q_i p_i(base) Geometric(y) = Π_i ( p_i(y) / p_i(base) ) ^ w_i, w_i = base-year cost share

Item contributions (chart 3) are each item's dollar change, q_i × Δp_i, so they add up exactly to the change in total cost. Comparisons with CPI test relative prices, not affordability: removing food from CPI changes the result by under one percentage point, so the comparison is not circular.

A7Affordability measures

Affordability compares the dinner with income rather than prices. All measures are national:

  • Hours of work at the average hourly earnings of production and nonsupervisory workers (AHETPI) and at the federal minimum wage (FEDMINNFRWG, chart 7).
  • Share of a week's median household income (MEHOINUSA646N).
  • Per-guest cost vs a day's income per person: disposable income (A229RC0) and "discretionary" income = disposable income minus personal spending on housing, utilities, food, health care and energy (BEA, Q3).
  • Household budgets by group (charts 4–6): BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey means by income fifth and by 14 household types. Essentials = shelter, utilities, food at home and health care (robustness: plus household operations incl. child care and vehicle insurance). Because the lowest fifth reports spending far above its income, total spending is used as the budget for fifths; changes use 3-year averages (2017–19 vs 2023–25).
Part B · Limitations

B1Observed, estimated and imputed prices

Every price in the index falls into one of three classes. The distinction matters: an observed price can be wrong only through survey error; an estimated or imputed price also carries model error.

Observed

Measured in a store survey

BLS October average prices (russet potatoes, milk, butter, eggs, flour, sugar; turkey to 2019). One-time anchor prices in 2026: the AFBF survey and cider listings. NYC: New York Post store prices for 2025 and 2026.

Estimated

Predicted by a validated model

Turkey, March 2020–October 2026, from the backtested ensemble (A4). Comes with an empirical error band (≈8% typical October error).

Imputed

Filled by an assumption

Anchor items before 2026 (CPI-scaled); green beans (pea proxy); butter 2012–18 (dairy CPI); October 2013 turkey and October 2025 all items (Sep/Nov average); NYC Post items before 2025 (index-scaled); NYC non-Post items (national × factor).

NYC series

In 2025 and 2026, 11 of 18 NYC items are observed Post prices (about 84% of the 2026 NYC cost, including the turkey). The rest are imputed as national × factor, and since national turkey and anchors are themselves estimated or imputed in some years, NYC history before 2025 is almost entirely imputed.

B2Price limitations

  1. HighGroup indexes miss item-specific swings. Anchor items follow broad CPI groups (bakery, fruits & vegetables, dairy, beverages). A bad cranberry harvest or a canned-pumpkin shortage would not show. Errors largely cancel across items but can be large for a single item.
  2. MediumOne-time anchors. Each anchor rests on one survey or four listings in one year. A sale or regional quirk in that observation shifts the item's entire 32-year history by the same proportion.
  3. MediumOctober, not Thanksgiving week. BLS prices are October averages. Holiday promotions (turkey loss leaders especially) and late-November spikes are not captured; this is the main reason the AFBF turkey diverges in 2025–26.
  4. MediumNational averages. BLS average prices mix store types, brands and regions. No household pays exactly these prices; low-income households may pay more (fewer large stores) or less (discount stores, store brands).
  5. LowFixed menu and quantities. The basket does not substitute when prices change (e.g., fewer eggs during bird flu), which slightly overstates cost increases. The geometric index partly addresses this.
  6. LowScope. The menu omits alcohol, appetizers, non-food items (foil, disposable pans), travel and cooking energy, so it understates the full cost of hosting.
  7. LowShutdown fills and bridges. The Sep/Nov average for October 2013 and October 2025, and the dairy-CPI bridge for butter 2012–18, assume smooth movement through the gap.
  8. LowGreen-bean proxy. No direct price exists; frozen peas are used at the same price. The item is $1.99 of $87.

B3Turkey model limitations

  1. HighAbout 8% typical error on the largest item. October error averages 8.3% and reached 26% in the worst backtest October. On a $34 turkey line, that is roughly ±$3 typically and up to ±$9 in a bad year, against a 2025→26 national change of $0.18.
  2. HighDivergence from the AFBF in 2025–26. The model puts turkey at +33% and +35% vs 2019; AFBF shows +3% and +14%. The two measure different things (regular shelf price vs volunteer-recorded holiday sale price), but there is no published BLS price to settle it. With the AFBF turkey the 2026 national dinner is $82.09; with the chicken ratio, $91.93.
  3. MediumThe past may not resemble the gap. Backtests span 2004–2019. The 2020–26 period had a pandemic, unusual inflation and the largest avian-flu outbreak on record; the error band assumes errors behave as in the backtests.
  4. MediumPost-hoc model choice. The combination was adopted after seeing backtest results (disclosed in A4). It also wins under the original rule, which limits but does not remove the selection effect.
  5. LowFew independent tests. Four backtest origins with overlapping horizons give 2,219 errors but far fewer independent Octobers; Diebold-Mariano results should be read as indicative.

B4New York City limitations

  1. HighThe Post's changes are far larger than official indexes. Milk +50% vs +0.9%, cider +75% vs +3.5%, pumpkin +43% vs +4.9%. A news survey of a few stores, brands and dates is noisier than BLS; the 2025→26 NYC jump (+19%) should be treated with care.
  2. HighPremium and branded items. The Post priced Bell & Evans turkey ($3.99/lb) and name brands. A shopper buying a store-brand frozen turkey would pay much less, so the NYC total leans toward the upper-middle of the market.
  3. MediumNo store-level detail. Only the Post's per-item prices are available, not which store or how many stores each came from, so variance cannot be measured.
  4. MediumQuantity scaling. Some Post items are a close but different product (canned cranberry sauce for fresh cranberries, crescent rolls for dinner rolls, baby carrots for a veggie tray), scaled linearly to menu quantities.
  5. MediumThe NYC factor is broad. It covers all goods in a metro area that includes New Jersey and the suburbs, and is moved through time with a metro grocery CPI. It will understate prices in high-cost Manhattan stores and overstate them at outer-borough discounters.
  6. MediumNYC history is national history. Before 2025 NYC prices are backcast with national item indexes, so they assume NYC prices moved like U.S. prices.

B5Household-data limitations

  1. HighLow-income income is under-reported. The lowest fifth reports spending about twice its after-tax income (the bottom half, about 120% of pre-tax income), reflecting under-reported income, benefits and retirees drawing on savings. Shares of income for these groups are approximate.
  2. MediumSurvey changes. The Consumer Expenditure Survey began imputing missing income in 2004 and changed income questions in 2013; long-run comparisons of income shares cross these breaks (also checked within 2004–25 and 2013–25).
  3. MediumGroup means hide variation. A fifth contains households of very different sizes, locations and tenure. It also misses people without stable housing, the population most exposed to food costs.
  4. MediumNational, not NYC, budgets. NYC income bands were not built (the Census API key was unavailable), so charts 4–6 are national, and the dinner in charts 5 and 7 is priced at U.S. averages.
  5. LowFederal minimum wage only. Chart 7 uses the federal $7.25. Many states and cities (including New York) are much higher, so it shows the floor, not typical low-wage pay.
  6. LowGasoline excluded and national accounts include third-party payments. FRED's quintile gasoline series is broken from 2023; BEA health spending includes employer and government payments, which is why national essentials look flat.

B6Interpretation

  • Price vs affordability. "The dinner rose in line with CPI" is a statement about relative prices, not about whether households can afford it. Affordability is judged against wages and budgets (A7).
  • Year-to-year changes are within error. The 2025→26 national change ($0.18) is far smaller than the turkey model's typical error; only multi-year changes should be read as signal.
  • Not captured in annual data. The November 2025 shutdown delayed or cut SNAP benefits for millions of households just before Thanksgiving; this does not appear in any series used here.
